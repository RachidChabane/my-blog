I think artificial intelligence, the way it is arriving, is taking us toward a dystopia. I have been saying so to people around me since early 2025, when Anthropic released [Claude Code](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-3-7-sonnet) on 24 February, and I am writing it down now because I believe many people, including in my own trade, still look at these tools with blinkers on.

My reasoning has two parts. The first is technical. I want to show, with precise cases, that what a current model can do depends mostly on what someone bothered to plug into it, and that almost nobody has done that work yet. The second is not technical. I ask what all of this is for, and I have not found an answer that satisfies me.

## A terminal reaches everything that plugs in

Claude Code is a language model that was given a terminal. It types commands, reads what the machine answers and starts again until the job is done. It was presented as a tool for writing code, and that is how nearly everyone uses it. I took something else from it. From a terminal you command a whole computer, and a computer commands whatever is attached to it. A printer, a camera, a microscope, a robot arm or a factory machine all obey commands as soon as they have a port and a driver. So a device only has to connect to a computer for a model to be able to use it.

The question is no longer really whether the model is intelligent enough. It is whether someone did the wiring. That is plumbing, thankless and unspectacular, which is why so little is said about it. The few times it has been done, the result is worth a close look.

The Mars case says the most. On 8 and 10 December 2025, the Perseverance rover followed a route prepared by Claude Code. According to [Anthropic's account](https://www.anthropic.com/features/claude-on-mars) and [the Jet Propulsion Laboratory's](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-perseverance-rover-completes-first-ai-planned-drive-on-mars), the model cut the route into ten-metre segments and wrote the instructions itself. The engineers then checked the plan in their simulator, against more than 500,000 telemetry variables, and made only minor changes. Nobody built a model to drive on Mars. A programming tool was handed the file format the rover understands.

The quadruped robot tells the same story with a stopwatch. In [Anthropic's experiment](https://www.anthropic.com/research/project-fetch-phase-two), two teams of people with no robotics background had to get a store-bought robot working. On the four tasks everyone finished, the team without AI took 361 minutes, the team helped by the model 181 minutes, and the model alone 9 minutes and 35 seconds.

At the end of August 2026, Anthropic also published [a shared driver for laboratory and factory equipment](https://www.anthropic.com/news/model-hardware-standard-research-preview). The company writes that connecting an instrument to an agent used to take weeks or months, and now takes hours or minutes. Once the plumbing is a product, not much is left between the model and the workshop.

Not everything worked. The robot was supposed to end by fetching a ball, and it failed. In a Genentech laboratory, the model had to be taught that a foaming liquid is a physical failure and not a bug. The NASA engineers reviewed the plan before sending it. Those limits are real. I only note that they concern contact with matter, which is the part better sensors and better tools improve year after year.

## Our interfaces were made for us

I write software for a living. A large share of what we have built in fifty years exists for one reason, which is that humans have to find their way around. Menus, forms, dashboards, applications that talk to each other through carefully documented gateways, all of it makes legible what is not legible to us. A model has no such need. It reads the database, the logs and the source code directly, and that is enough for it.

What is arriving looks less like a new tool than like a new inhabitant, one that works without the intermediaries we turned into an industry. The vendors say it themselves. In April 2026, Salesforce announced [a version of its platform with no interface](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/salesforce-headless-360-announcement/), explaining that agents do not open a browser or click through screens, they call APIs and commands directly. The announcement quotes one of the founders asking why you should ever log into Salesforce again. The stock market had asked the same question before him. Between 12 January and 23 February 2026, after the release of a desktop agent and its plugins, the American index of software companies [lost 25%](https://www.deeplearning.ai/the-batch/claude-cowork-plugins-trigger-a-saas-stock-selloff-but-partnerships-lead-to-slight-rebound).

I conclude that a good share of the software we know will end up disappearing, and that we should ask plainly whether engineers will still be needed to write it. At Google, [75% of new code](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/infrastructure-and-cloud/google-cloud/cloud-next-2026-sundar-pichai/) is already produced by AI and then approved by engineers.

The employment figures do not say that yet, and I have to admit it. The Stanford researchers who follow the subject headline their [August 2026 update](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/) with the absence of widespread displacement. What they measure is narrower. Workers aged 22 to 25 in the most exposed occupations are about 19% fewer in employment than they would otherwise be, and the gap is widening because fewer beginners are hired. Job postings for developers have even [been rising since 2025](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/?p=20708), mostly for senior roles. You can read that as the trade holding up. I read it as a trade that has stopped training its successors, which is the quietest way to close a profession.

## Mathematicians thought they were safe

Mathematics was supposed to be the counterexample. A language model copies, the argument went, and a new proof cannot be copied. Mathematicians believed it themselves, and they are the ones now describing how they changed their minds.

In July 2025, a Google DeepMind model scored [35 points out of 42 at the International Mathematical Olympiad](https://deepmind.google/blog/advanced-version-of-gemini-with-deep-think-officially-achieves-gold-medal-standard-at-the-international-mathematical-olympiad/), gold-medal level, on papers marked by the competition's own graders. You could still answer that these are exercises for very gifted teenagers. In January 2026, an open problem posed by Paul Erdős was solved for the most part by a model, then verified by another program. In May 2026, OpenAI announced that one of its models had disproved a geometry conjecture Erdős had formulated in 1946 and that most specialists believed true. Nine mathematicians went through the result and [published their remarks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.20695). One of them, the Fields medallist Timothy Gowers, writes according to [Quanta](https://www.quantamagazine.org/why-the-legendary-erdos-problems-are-falling-to-ai-20260803/) that he would have recommended acceptance without any hesitation.

What the people concerned say tells more than the results. Ken Ono [recounts](https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/q-a-ken-ono-feared-ai-now-he-trains-it) that he was very skeptical and that he spent the summer of 2025 depressed. Daniel Litt had bet in March 2025, at three to one, that no AI would write on its own a paper at the level of the best before 2030. Less than a year later he [writes](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/02/daniel-litt-on-ai-and-math.html) that he expects to lose. Noga Alon, who solved a few dozen Erdős problems over his career, has stopped looking for more. He tells Quanta there is no point anymore now that AI has started on them.

And then there is Riemann. The hypothesis he formulated in 1859 is held to be the most famous problem in mathematics. In August 2026, a research version of Claude raised from 41.6% to 67.2% the share of zeros that can be proved to lie where the hypothesis expects them, and [Scientific American](https://scientificamerican.com/article/no-ai-didnt-just-solve-the-thorniest-problem-in-math) was right to point out that the problem itself had not moved. In October, OpenAI published a proof of a weakened version of the hypothesis, which the specialists [Quanta](https://www.quantamagazine.org/ai-proved-the-quasi-riemann-hypothesis-what-happens-now-20261019/) spoke to largely consider correct. On 20 November 2026, five days ago, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, announced a proof of the generalized hypothesis, obtained with Claude. It runs to more than 17 million lines of Lean, a language in which a program checks every step of the reasoning, and there is no version a human can read. According to [The Conversation](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-sacred-mathematics-riemann-hypothesis-ai.html), the teams that maintain Lean and its mathematics library have stated that they believe it is correct, and Daniel Litt considers it exceedingly likely to be correct and understood by no one yet. So it is not confirmed in the usual sense, the one where mathematicians have read it and understood it.

It would be dishonest to stop there. In February 2026, eleven mathematicians gave the models ten unpublished problems. The models returned a confident proof for every one, and [only two were correct](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/first-proof-is-ais-toughest-math-test-yet-the-results-are-mixed/). A "discovery" from Christmas 2025 turned out to be a result Erdős had published himself in 1977. Ono also says this is not the end of mathematics. I believe him. But the bet of two years ago was that this field would stay out of reach, and that bet is lost.

## Medicine is only late

Medicine believes itself safe in the same way. I think it is only late, and its lateness is a matter of wiring. A diagnosis starts from measurements, a blood test, an image, a trace, the patient's answers. All of these devices already produce digital data, and sending it to a model instead of a screen requires no invention.

When someone does, the figures look like the ones from mathematics. In June 2025, Microsoft [gave its system](https://microsoft.ai/news/the-path-to-medical-superintelligence/) 304 hard cases published by the New England Journal of Medicine. It found the right diagnosis in 85.5% of them, against 20% on average for 21 experienced physicians. Those physicians worked without colleagues, books or tools, which is not how they usually work, and Microsoft points out that its system is not approved for clinical use. In March 2026, Google tested its own [in a real urgent care clinic](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08448), with one hundred patients and under physicians' supervision. The right diagnosis was on its list in 90% of cases.

Practice follows, slowly. The American drug regulator lists [more than 1,600 medical devices that use AI](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence/artificial-intelligence-enabled-medical-devices). The state of Utah [has let a system renew prescriptions](https://www.kuer.org/health/2026-10-22/utah-ai-prescription-pilot-doctronics) for long-term treatments since January 2026, though the trial is still in the phase where a physician reviews each one. Surgery seems further off, and yet a robot at Johns Hopkins University [carried out on its own](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.10251v3) one stage of a gallbladder removal on eight pig organs, without a failure and correcting itself along the way. It was not a living patient, and a human changed the instruments.

## The two barriers that remain

The first barrier comes from us. We do not want to be treated by a machine, and the law reflects that. According to [a Gallup survey from August 2026](https://news.gallup.com/poll/716261/providers-underestimate-patient-comfort-care.aspx), 9% of Americans would be comfortable with an AI deciding their treatment with no review. In Europe, [the AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/) requires that a person be able to oversee and stop these systems. I do not believe this barrier will hold for long. The same survey shows that 56% of people already accept the AI deciding as long as the provider can overrule it, and the providers surveyed thought that number was far lower.

The second barrier is physical. A surgical gesture or the driving of a machine means reacting in a fraction of a second and aiming true, and current models take several seconds to answer. The network is already not the obstacle. In March 2026, a London surgeon [operated on a patient in Gibraltar](https://www.techspot.com/news/111595-london-doctor-carries-out-remote-robot-surgery-cancer.html) with 60 milliseconds between his movement and the robot's. For speed, I am not counting on the same models made faster. I expect it to come from systems designed differently. For accuracy, the model does not need a steady hand. A scanner can already capture the shape of a part or an organ in three dimensions, and a machine can go to a point once it is given the coordinates. The precision is then the instrument's, and instruments improve without making noise. I see no obstacle of principle there, only delays.

## A production with no goal

I come to what really concerns me. Suppose everything above happens. What will we have gained? We call it progress, and I would like someone to tell me toward what.

While these models learn to drive rovers, here is where the simplest needs stand.

I know the objection. This money does not sit in the same pocket, hunger also comes from wars and failed states, and a model able to make a diagnosis may well be useful in a village with no doctor. All of that is true. The fact remains that we can raise hundreds of billions in a few months for data centres, and that over the same period official development aid [fell by 23% in 2025](https://sdg.iisd.org/news/oda-in-historic-decline-latest-oecd-data/), its largest drop on record. We have the means. What the comparison shows is what we choose to do with them.

In 1930, Keynes [predicted](https://quoteinvestigator.com/?p=448841) that a hundred years on, the standard of living in rich countries would be four to eight times higher, and that a fifteen-hour week would be enough. He was right about the wealth. As for the rest, nobody ever decided that we had enough. That is why AI looks to me like capitalism reaching its end point. When you produce without having set a goal, the only thing left to do is produce faster, and a machine that produces without us is where that logically ends.

My distance comes from further back than economics. Our time here is short. Each of us has a counted number of days and takes nothing of what he accumulated with him. A life has an end, so it can have a measure, the measure of what it takes to live with dignity and of what we owe those who do not have that. A system that cannot say the word "enough" runs against something that plain, and it carries all of us along. I do not see how to call progress a movement that has no end point and no relation to the length of our lives.

## Why I still work in it

You will answer that I spend my days building with these tools, and that is correct. I had not planned it. I went into computing for a quiet trade, and AI arrived faster than I imagined. I carry on because it is what I know how to do and one has to live on something. I do not believe you leave a system by refusing to earn your living in it.

What I expect from it for myself is modest. I would like, one day, to depend a little less on all this, to live more simply and further from screens. That wish has at least one quality the system lacks, it has an end. The day I have what I need, I will not look for more.

## What I do not know

I do not know how fast all this will go. The cases I cited are demonstrations, often told by the people who sell the model, and between a demonstration and a hospital ten years can pass as easily as two. I do not know either whether the collapse of software will be sudden or will take the form of a slow erosion that the statistics take a long time to show.
